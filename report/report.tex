\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem}

A continuous belt produces $x \times y$ boxes (with $y$ orthogonal to the
belt direction). A target packaging layer of $n$ boxes is given by the
destination positions $(P_x[i], P_y[i])$ for $i \in 1,\dots,n$, so that boxes
do not overlap and tile a rectangle.

A robotic gripper of size $S$ can pick a contiguous run of boxes along the
$x$-axis whose total physical length is at most $S + x$ (i.e. up to
$\lfloor (S+x)/x \rfloor$ boxes), with at most $x/2$ overflow per side. The
gripper has two parallel plates: only \emph{one} can open at any release, so
the side that opens must face a free direction --- either the boundary or a
position that has not been filled yet. This generates two coupled subproblems:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item \textbf{Grouping.} Partition the $n$ packs into contiguous,
        same-row groups, each fitting in the gripper.
  \item \textbf{Scheduling.} Order the groups in time and assign a plate
        side per group so that, when released, the opening plate faces an
        empty direction.
\end{enumerate}

We encode (a) and (b) jointly in a single MiniZinc model, solved as a
satisfiability problem.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

The MiniZinc model lives in \texttt{model.mzn}. We describe its parameters,
decision variables, and constraints in detail.

\subsection{Parameters and derived constants}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Parameters and derived constants.}]
par int: n_packs;
par int: packs_x_dim;
par int: packs_along_x;
par int: packs_along_y;
par int: grip_size;

par int: max_pkble_packs = (grip_size + packs_x_dim) div packs_x_dim;
par int: groups_per_row  = (packs_along_x + max_pkble_packs - 1)
                            div max_pkble_packs;
par int: n_groups        = packs_along_y * groups_per_row;
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Inputs.} \texttt{n\_packs} is the total number of boxes on the
pallet. \texttt{packs\_along\_x} and \texttt{packs\_along\_y} are the grid
dimensions of the placement layer. \texttt{packs\_x\_dim} is the box side
parallel to the gripper (so \texttt{packs\_x\_dim}~$= x$ in the natural
orientation, or $y$ if the layer is rotated). \texttt{grip\_size} is $S$.

\paragraph{Derived.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{max\_pkble\_packs} $= \lfloor (S + x)/x \rfloor$ is the
        gripper capacity in boxes, derived directly from the geometry.
  \item \texttt{groups\_per\_row} is the minimum number of groups needed
        to cover one row, computed by ceiling division.
  \item \texttt{n\_groups} fixes the total number of groups. We use a
        \emph{fixed} group cardinality to make the model fully static:
        every group is filled, and the partition is forced to be a
        contiguous prefix-sum sequence.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Decision variables}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Decision variables.}]
array[1..n_groups] of var 1..n_packs:         group_start;
array[1..n_groups] of var 1..max_pkble_packs: group_len;

array[1..n_groups] of var 1..n_groups: drop_order;
array[1..n_groups] of var 0..1:        plate;
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{group\_start[g]} -- index of the first pack of group $g$
        in row-major order over the pallet grid.
  \item \texttt{group\_len[g]} -- number of packs in group $g$,
        bounded by the gripper capacity.
  \item \texttt{drop\_order[g]} -- the temporal step (1..n\_groups) at
        which group $g$ is dropped on the pallet.
  \item \texttt{plate[g]} -- which plate opens when group $g$ is released
        (0 = left plate, 1 = right plate).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Grouping constraints}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Contiguity and row-boundary constraints.}]
constraint group_start[1] = 1;

constraint forall(i in 1..n_groups - 1)(
  group_start[i + 1] = group_start[i] + group_len[i]
);

constraint group_start[n_groups] + group_len[n_groups] - 1 = n_packs;

constraint forall(i in 1..n_groups)(
  (group_start[i] - 1)                div packs_along_x =
  (group_start[i] + group_len[i] - 2) div packs_along_x
);
\end{lstlisting}

The first three constraints together force the groups to form a contiguous
partition of $\{1,\dots,n\}$: the first group starts at pack 1, each
subsequent group starts where the previous one ended, and the last group
ends at pack $n$. This makes \texttt{group\_start} a prefix-sum of
\texttt{group\_len}, which gives the solver a tight propagation handle.

The fourth constraint enforces the \emph{same-row} requirement of the
gripper: a group must not straddle a row boundary in the row-major
indexing. Using integer division by \texttt{packs\_along\_x}, the row index
of the first pack must equal the row index of the last pack.

\subsection{Neighbor structure (parameter, not variable)}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Left/right neighbors as par arrays.}]
array[1..n_groups] of par int: left_neighbor =
  [ if (g - 1) mod groups_per_row > 0
    then g - 1 else 0 endif
  | g in 1..n_groups ];

array[1..n_groups] of par int: right_neighbor =
  [ if (g - 1) mod groups_per_row < groups_per_row - 1
    then g + 1 else 0 endif
  | g in 1..n_groups ];
\end{lstlisting}

Because groups are filled in row-major order with a fixed
\texttt{groups\_per\_row}, the spatial adjacency between groups is
\emph{known at compile time}: it is a function of the group index alone, not
of \texttt{group\_start} or \texttt{group\_len}. We exploit this by declaring
\texttt{left\_neighbor} and \texttt{right\_neighbor} as \texttt{par} (not
\texttt{var}) arrays, computed from index arithmetic. A neighbor index of
$0$ is the sentinel for ``no neighbor on that side'' (i.e. row boundary).
This removes a layer of decision variables and lets the flattener fully
unroll the scheduling constraint below.

\subsection{Scheduling constraints}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Plate-collision constraint and step permutation.}]
constraint alldifferent(drop_order);

constraint forall(g in 1..n_groups)(
  (plate[g] = 0 /\
    (left_neighbor[g]  = 0 \/
     drop_order[left_neighbor[g]]  > drop_order[g]))
  \/
  (plate[g] = 1 /\
    (right_neighbor[g] = 0 \/
     drop_order[right_neighbor[g]] > drop_order[g]))
);

solve satisfy;
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Permutation.} \texttt{alldifferent(drop\_order)} together with
the domain $1..n\_groups$ forces \texttt{drop\_order} to be a permutation:
each step $1,\dots,n\_groups$ is used exactly once.

\paragraph{Plate-collision.} For each group $g$, we require that the
\emph{opening} plate faces a free side at the moment of release. ``Free''
means either:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the relevant neighbor does not exist (sentinel $0$, group is on a
        row boundary), or
  \item the neighbor exists but is dropped \emph{later} than $g$, so the
        cell on that side is still empty.
\end{itemize}
Encoding this as a disjunction over \texttt{plate[g]} couples the
two scheduling decisions: once \texttt{plate} is fixed for a group, the
required temporal ordering with one specific neighbor is determined.

\paragraph{Solve.} The model is a pure feasibility problem (\texttt{solve
satisfy}); we do not optimize the schedule, since any ordering that
respects the plate-collision constraint is a valid pick-and-place plan.

\section{Driving the model from Python}
\label{sec:python}

The Python layer is intentionally thin --- it is a wrapper around
\texttt{minizinc-python} that handles the geometry computations the model
deliberately leaves out, and produces visual artifacts of solutions. Three
scripts compose the pipeline:

\begin{description}
  \item[\texttt{sample.py}] Solves a single instance and renders the
        layout and a step-by-step drop schedule as PDFs. The Python side
        is responsible for picking the orientation that fits more boxes
        on the pallet (\texttt{lib/utils.py}: \texttt{gen\_pallet\_info}),
        deriving \texttt{packs\_x\_dim} and the gripper size $S = 3x$,
        and translating the model output (the four arrays
        \texttt{group\_start}, \texttt{group\_len}, \texttt{drop\_order},
        \texttt{plate}) into a high-level \emph{schedule} of drop
        actions.
  \item[\texttt{benchmark.py}] Runs the same model across the cartesian
        product of five pallet presets and six pack sizes, against every
        available solver, with a 5-minute timeout. Each run is
        encapsulated and timing/error information is recorded in a
        single \texttt{benchmark/results.json} file.
  \item[\texttt{plot\_benchmark.py}] Consumes the JSON benchmark and
        produces the comparison plots referenced in
        Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}.
\end{description}

The renderer in \texttt{lib/visualize.py} is orientation-agnostic: it
consumes already-rotated cell dimensions (\texttt{cell\_w}, \texttt{cell\_h})
and the four solution arrays, and outputs both an overall pallet figure
(colored by group) and one figure per drop step (with an arrow showing the
opening plate side).

\subsection{Worked example}
\label{sec:example}

To make the output of the model concrete, we run \texttt{sample.py} on a
$70 \times 70$ pack and an $800 \times 1200$ pallet. The geometry helper
chooses the rotation that maximises pack count, yielding an
$11 \times 17$ grid (\(n = 187\) packs). With \(S = 3 \cdot 70 = 210\),
the gripper capacity is \(\lfloor (210 + 70)/70 \rfloor = 4\) packs, and
each row of 11 packs is split into 3 groups of sizes $3+4+4$, for a
total of \(n\_groups = 51\).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The two-stage formulation --- grouping the pallet into pickable runs, then
scheduling the drops with a plate side per group --- maps cleanly onto
MiniZinc decision variables and yields a compact, fully feasible model. The
key modelling choice is to fix the number of groups statically and force
the partition into a prefix-sum shape; this lets us pre-compute the
neighbor structure as parameters rather than variables, keeping the
plate-collision constraint a simple disjunction over indexed lookups.

The benchmark confirms that the model scales well across realistic pallet
sizes within the 5-minute budget, and exposes the expected per-solver
trade-offs between flattening overhead and search performance.
