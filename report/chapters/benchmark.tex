For each combination, the model is tested with four solvers:
\texttt{gecode}, \texttt{cp-sat}, \texttt{chuffed}, and \texttt{highs}.
These solvers represent and solve the problem in different ways.
\texttt{gecode} and \texttt{chuffed} mainly rely on constraint propagation,
\texttt{cp-sat} uses a SAT-based representation, and
\texttt{highs} uses a linear programming representation. Each run is executed
with a timeout of 300 seconds.
The benchmark considers three gripper sizes, $S \in \{1,2,3\}\cdot \texttt{boxes\_x\_dim},$
allowing us to compare how the gripper size affects chunking and
releasing performance.

\subsection{Scaling by gripper size}
The first comparison fixes the gripper multiplier and measures how the 
solve time changes with the total number of boxes. Figure~\ref{fig:scaling-per-grip}
shows the resulting scaling curves for $k=1$, $k=2$, and $k=3$.
This analysis captures the effect of the gripper size on the proposed model.
Larger grippers reduce the number of chunks that must be scheduled.
Therefore, the solve time generally decreases as the multiplier increases.

In particular, \texttt{gecode} and \texttt{chuffed} remain in the lowest
runtime range and show the best scaling behaviour. \texttt{cp-sat} shows
a clearer dependence on the number of boxes, but remains well below the
timeout. \texttt{highs} is consistently the slowest solver and reaches the
timeout range on the larger instances.
\subsection{Overall solver behaviour}

After separating the gripper sizes, we aggregate all benchmark runs into a
single scaling plot. Figure~\ref{fig:scaling-overall} reports the solve time as
a function of the total number of boxes, considering all solvers and all tested
instances. This view highlights the global trend across the benchmark and makes
the relative behaviour of the solvers easier to compare.

Overall, the comparison confirms that the propagation-based solvers are the most
suitable for this model. \texttt{gecode} and \texttt{chuffed} remain close to
the bottom of the plot for most instances, with \texttt{gecode} showing slightly
more regular behaviour. \texttt{chuffed} is also competitive, but its performance
is less regular. \texttt{cp-sat} shows a stronger dependence on the instance
size, although it still remains far from the timeout in most runs. In contrast,
\texttt{highs} performs worst and several runs approach or reach the timeout.

\subsection{Per-pallet breakdown}
Another useful comparison studies how the pallet preset influences solver
runtime. Differences across presets reflect how \texttt{boxes\_per\_row} and
\texttt{pallet\_rows} determine the number and arrangement of chunks.
Figure~\ref{fig:scaling-per-pallet} shows the resulting breakdown.

The relative behaviour of the solvers remains mostly unchanged across pallets:
\texttt{gecode} and \texttt{chuffed} stay close to the bottom of the plots,
\texttt{cp-sat} is slower but remains well below \texttt{highs}, and
\texttt{highs} is usually the slowest. Larger presets, especially the Industrie,
US, and Australian pallets, make the separation between solvers more visible
because they include denser instances. The half-pallet instances are smaller,
so the differences are less pronounced.

\subsection{Flattening and execution overhead}

The last analysis compares the solver-reported time with the total runtime
measured by the Python benchmark script. This comparison estimates the cost of
the steps that occur before the actual solver search. The main one is
MiniZinc flattening: in this phase, the high-level MiniZinc model and the
instance data are translated into a lower-level FlatZinc representation, which
is then passed to the selected backend solver. The remaining overhead includes
Python instance construction, MiniZinc invocation, and process startup.

Figure~\ref{fig:wall-vs-solve} shows the resulting comparison. The diagonal line
marks equal total runtime and solver-reported time. Points close to the diagonal
correspond to runs where most of the time is spent inside the solver. Points
above the diagonal instead indicate a larger overhead outside the solver; in
these cases, flattening and execution costs are more relevant relative to the
actual solving time.

The plot shows that this effect is strongest for the fastest runs, especially
for \texttt{gecode} and several \texttt{chuffed} executions. Their
solver-reported time is very small, so even a modest overhead produces a
visible separation from the diagonal. For slower runs, especially
\texttt{highs} and the larger \texttt{cp-sat} instances, the points are closer
to the diagonal. In those cases, the solving phase dominates the total runtime,
and the relative overhead becomes less important.
