For each combination, the model is tested with four solvers:
\texttt{gecode}, \texttt{cp-sat}, \texttt{chuffed}, and \texttt{highs}.
These solvers represent and solve the problem in different ways.
\texttt{gecode} and \texttt{chuffed} mainly rely on constraint propagation,
\texttt{cp-sat} uses a SAT-based representation, and
\texttt{highs} uses a linear programming representation. Each run is executed
with a timeout of 300 seconds.
The benchmark considers three gripper sizes, $S \in \{1,2,3\}\cdot \texttt{boxes\_x\_dim},$
allowing us to compare how the gripper size affects grouping and
scheduling performance.

\subsection{Scaling by gripper size}
The first comparison fixes the gripper multiplier and measures how the
solve time changes with the total number of boxes. Figure~\ref{fig:scaling-per-grip}
shows the resulting scaling curves for $k=1$, $k=2$, and $k=3$.
This analysis captures the effect of the gripper size on the proposed model.
Larger grippers reduce the number of groups that must be scheduled:
the average number of groups decreases from about $75$ for $k=1$
to about $40$ for $k=3$. Therefore, the solve time generally
decreases as the multiplier increases.

In particular, \texttt{gecode} remains in the lowest runtime range and
shows the best scaling behaviour. \texttt{cp-sat} shows a clearer
dependence on the number of boxes, but remains well below the timeout.
\texttt{chuffed} is usually fast, although it times out on the densest
instance for $k=1$. \texttt{highs} is consistently the slowest solver.

\subsection{Overall solver behaviour}

After separating the gripper sizes, we aggregate all benchmark runs into a
single scaling plot. Figure~\ref{fig:scaling-overall} reports the solve time as
a function of the total number of boxes, considering all solvers and all tested
instances. This view highlights the global trend across the benchmark and makes
the relative behaviour of the solvers easier to compare.

Overall, the comparison confirms that the propagation-based solvers are the most
suitable for this model. \texttt{gecode} gives the most stable performance and
remains close to the bottom of the plot for almost all instances. \texttt{chuffed}
is also competitive, but its performance is less regular. \texttt{cp-sat}
shows a stronger dependence on the instance size, although it still remains
far from the timeout in most runs. In contrast, \texttt{highs}
performs worst and several runs approach or reach the timeout.

\subsection{Per-pallet breakdown}
Another useful comparison studies how the pallet preset influences solver
runtime. Differences across presets reflect how \texttt{boxes\_along\_x} and
\texttt{boxes\_along\_y} determine the number and arrangement of groups.
Figure~\ref{fig:scaling-per-pallet} shows the resulting breakdown.

The relative behaviour of the solvers remains mostly unchanged across pallets:
\texttt{gecode} stays the most stable, \texttt{cp-sat} and \texttt{chuffed}
remain competitive, and \texttt{highs} is usually the slowest. Larger presets,
especially the Industrie, US, and Australian pallets, make the separation between
solvers more visible because they include denser instances. The half-pallet
instances are smaller, so the differences are less pronounced.
